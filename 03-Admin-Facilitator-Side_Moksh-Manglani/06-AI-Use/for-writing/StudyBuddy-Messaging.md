## 1. Elevator pitch (locked)

Approximately 55 seconds. Figures are sourced from Moksh's April literature review, except the reading figure, which was updated on 5 Oct (see sources below).

> UNESCO says the world needs 44 million more teachers by 2030. And seven in ten children in poorer countries still can't read a simple text by age ten.
>
> The schools that feel it hardest are rural and low income, where the good teachers leave for better pay. I've seen it happen. A school hires someone, trains them up, gets them good, and then a richer school hires them away. The kids get left behind.
>
> So what if the teaching didn't have to come from the person in the room?
>
> Think about it this way. Everyone knows one plus one is two. Almost nobody can teach it to a six-year-old. Knowing and teaching are different skills, and only teaching is scarce. So what if we have facilitators, people who can answer questions, while we take care of the teaching.
>
> An expert uses videos, worksheets, activities and resources to build a structured lesson. The facilitator goes through that lesson in class on a big screen, clicking through, playing videos, running the activities from the lesson plan, and helping whoever's stuck. The facilitator facilitates learning. The platform does the teaching.
>
> This is similar to an Uber, but for schools. Running a classroom doesn't need a teaching degree anymore. And the students still get the expert.

### Delivery notes

- Pause before "Everyone knows one plus one is two." That is where the room turns.
- "The facilitator facilitates learning. The platform does the teaching." is the thesis in eleven words. Land on it.
- The three figures each do a job: 44 million is scale, the reading figure is "worse than you think", and the self-directed learning point below is the defence against "why not just send the videos home".
- The Uber line: good for business and investor audiences, risky for academic ones. At the conference and Display Day, consider cutting straight to "running a classroom doesn't need a teaching degree anymore". It also sits awkwardly beside the softened commercialisation answer in the ethics application.
- "I've seen it happen" is deliberately non-specific. In an ethics conversation the same relationship is the conflict of interest under review, so the framing changes by room.

### Sources behind the figures

- UNESCO (2024), Global report on teachers: 44 million additional primary and secondary teachers needed worldwide by 2030.
- World Bank (2022), The State of Global Learning Poverty: 2022 Update: an estimated 70% of 10-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries cannot read and understand a simple text. (Updated 5 Oct 2026. The earlier 53% was the first estimate from 2015 data, later revised to 57% pre-pandemic. Source: https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2022/06/23/70-of-10-year-olds-now-in-learning-poverty-unable-to-read-and-understand-a-simple-text)
- Jordan (2015): MOOC completion rates often below 15%. Chen (2009), Kim et al. (2014): learners need strong self-regulation to benefit from independent digital learning, which K-12 learners largely lack. This is the evidence base for keeping a human in the room.

## 2. How the system actually works

Corrected 14 September. Earlier framing had the facilitator delivering the lesson. That is wrong.

**The platform teaches. The facilitator runs the room.**

The video and the activities deliver the instruction to the students directly. The facilitator advances the steps, keeps classroom order, prints and distributes worksheets, answers small questions, and supports individual students. The facilitator does not teach.

This matters because it changes the central research claim. The question is not "can a non-expert deliver a lesson well". It is "can instruction be delivered by the platform, with a human present for supervision and support only". The second is a stronger and more defensible claim.

## 3. The three-layer quality argument (locked)

Replaces the earlier "training versus supporting" framing. Gill asked Moksh to say he *trains* facilitators. This is more accurate and every layer is enforced in code rather than promised.

> StudyBuddy controls quality at three points, not one.
>
> **Screening.** Nobody self-enrols as a facilitator. A person requests access, and an administrator reviews that request and decides whether to grant it, and to which classes. Who ends up in front of students is an explicit human decision, made by someone accountable for the school.
>
> **Induction.** Once approved, a facilitator cannot deliver a lesson they have not walked through. The first pass locks each step until the previous one is complete, so they meet the lesson's shape, sequence and materials before they meet the class.
>
> **Support.** From then on the lesson is unlocked, and the platform holds the structure, delivers the instruction, and places a route back to the lesson's author where a facilitator is most likely to need it.
>
> So the platform does not train a teacher, and it does not simply hand someone a document. It filters who gets in, rehearses them once, and carries the teaching thereafter.

## 4. Two objections to have answers ready for

**"If the platform does the teaching, why not just send the videos home?"**

Because self-paced content fails for this age group, which is the finding in Moksh's own literature review. MOOC completion under 15% (Jordan, 2015); learners need self-regulation skills that K-12 students largely do not have (Chen, 2009; Kim et al., 2014). The human is in the room precisely because content alone does not work for children. Structured content plus human supervision, where neither works alone.

**"Isn't this just a flipped classroom, or Bridge International?"**

No. A flipped classroom sends content home and uses class time for practice. Bridge International scripts a human teacher reading from a tablet. Here the instruction happens in class, delivered by the platform, with a human present for management and support only. Different from both.
